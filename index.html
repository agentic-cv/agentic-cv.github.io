Introduction

AI agents are rapidly transforming computer vision. By equipping multimodal foundation models with tools such as renderers, reconstruction pipelines, and 3D modeling software, researchers can build systems that reconstruct scenes, simulate physical phenomena, and edit 3D assets without task-specific training. These emerging capabilities are exciting, but they also raise fundamental questions. How much of the progress comes from new algorithms, and how much comes from increasingly powerful foundation models? How can we ensure reproducibility when the underlying models are constantly evolving? And how should we evaluate progress when agents can iteratively explore solutions and refine their outputs?

This workshop brings together researchers to discuss the opportunities, challenges, and future directions of agentic computer vision. We will focus on three central questions:

  • What becomes possible? How can agents that interact with tools, observe results, and iteratively refine their actions advance computer vision tasks such as 3D reconstruction, vision reasoning, perception, generation, editing, and simulation?
  • What are the challenges? How should we assess scientific novelty, measure meaningful progress, and ensure reproducibility when much of an agent’s capability comes from its underlying foundation model?
  • How should the computer vision community respond? What research directions should we prioritize? How should we adapt our evaluation and peer-review practices? And what skills should we teach the next generation of researchers?

Call for papers

The call for papers will open soon. We welcome contributions on both sides of the debate.

  • Papers that demonstrate the opportunity. Agentic systems that advance computer vision tasks such as 3D reconstruction, perception, generation, editing, and simulation, as well as agents that serve as research tools by running experiments, curating data, or constructing benchmarks.
  • Papers that examine the limits. Studies of the limitations and blind spots of current agents, such as failures in spatial reasoning, weak verification, or high computational cost, together with analyses of the risks that agents pose to the research community, including irreproducible results, benchmark gaming, unclear attribution, and growing strain on peer review.

We especially encourage position papers, negative results, and reproduction studies in both categories.

  • Submissions are short papers of up to 4 pages, excluding references, in the CVPR format.
  • Accepted papers are non-archival and will not appear in the proceedings, so authors remain free to submit their work to archival venues.
  • We also welcome recently accepted papers from other venues for presentation at the poster session.
  • Reviewing is double-blind and conducted through OpenReview.
  • Accepted papers will be presented in the poster session.

Invited speakers

We plan five to six invited talks, roughly two for each of the three questions, from speakers who hold different views on the question in the title. Their talks set the stage for the closing debate. More names will be announced as they are confirmed.

  • Andrea Vedaldi University of Oxford

    Andrea Vedaldi is Professor of Computer Vision and Machine Learning at the University of Oxford, where he co-leads the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) in the Department of Engineering Science. He is also a Research Scientist and Technical Lead at Meta, working on 3D computer vision and generative AI, and an Amazon Scholar in the Frontier AI and Robotics lab. His research develops computer vision and machine learning methods that interpret the semantic content and the 3D geometry of images and videos with minimal human labeling, with a current focus on spatial intelligence. His honors include a Royal Society Faraday Discovery Fellowship, the IEEE Thomas Huang Memorial Prize, the IEEE Mark Everingham Prize, two CVPR Best Paper Awards, and an ACM Test of Time Award. He is a Fellow of the Royal Academy of Engineering and of ELLIS, and has served as a chair of ECCV.

  • Rana Hanocka University of Chicago

    Rana Hanocka is an Assistant Professor of Computer Science at the University of Chicago and the Founder and CEO of Thrixel. She leads 3DL (threedle!), a group of researchers working on 3D, machine learning, and visual computing. She obtained her Ph.D. in 2021 from Tel Aviv University under the supervision of Daniel Cohen-Or and Raja Giryes. Her research builds artificial intelligence for 3D data, spanning computer graphics, machine learning, and computer vision, with the goal of bringing the success of deep learning on structured data to unstructured 3D geometry. Her group developed MeshCNN, a convolutional neural network designed specifically for meshes, and explored learning from the internal data of a single shape for surface reconstruction, geometric texture synthesis, and point cloud consolidation. Her current interests include intertwining human and machine creativity in 3D modeling and animation, learning with less supervision from large shape collections, and making 3D neural networks more interpretable.
