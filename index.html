Introduction

Agents arrived in computer vision almost overnight. Give a multimodal model a renderer, a reconstruction pipeline, or a copy of Blender, and it will reconstruct a scene, fit a simulation, or edit a 3D asset without a single task-specific weight. The results are hard to ignore. So are the doubts. Where does the gain come from, the method or the model underneath it? Can anyone reproduce a result built on a model that changes every quarter? What does a benchmark measure once an agent can search for the answer?

This workshop is a debate, not a showcase. We will put builders and skeptics on the same stage, ask them to argue, and let the audience vote. Three questions set the agenda.

  • What becomes possible? Which vision problems, from 3D reconstruction and perception to generation, editing, and simulation, open up when a model can act, look, and try again?
  • What do we risk? How do we judge novelty, measure progress, and keep results reproducible when the foundation model does most of the work?
  • How should the vision community respond? What should we work on, how should we review, and what should the next generation of students learn?

Call for papers

The call for papers will open soon. We invite papers on both sides of the debate.

  • Papers that show the opportunity. Agentic systems that solve computer vision problems such as 3D reconstruction, perception, generation, editing, and simulation, and agents that serve as research tools, for example by running experiments, curating data, or building benchmarks.
  • Papers that show the limits. Limitations and blind spots of current agents, such as failures of spatial reasoning, weak verification, or high cost, and the risks or adverse impact of agents on the vision research community, such as irreproducible results, benchmark gaming, unclear credit, and strain on reviewing.

Position papers, negative results, and reproduction studies are welcome in both categories.

  • We accept short papers of up to 4 pages, excluding references, in the CVPR format.
  • Papers are non-archival and will not appear in the proceedings, so authors can later submit them to archival venues.
  • Recently accepted papers from other venues are welcome for poster presentation.
  • Reviewing is double-blind and runs on OpenReview.
  • Accepted papers are presented in the poster session.

Invited speakers

We plan five to six invited talks, about two for each topic, by speakers who hold different views on the question in the title. The talks set up the closing debate. More names will appear here as they are confirmed.

  • Andrea Vedaldi University of Oxford

    Andrea Vedaldi is Professor of Computer Vision and Machine Learning at the University of Oxford, where he co-leads the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) in the Department of Engineering Science. He is also a Research Scientist and Technical Lead at Meta, working on 3D computer vision and generative AI, and an Amazon Scholar in the Frontier AI and Robotics lab. His research develops computer vision and machine learning methods that interpret the semantic content and the 3D geometry of images and videos with minimal human labeling, with a current focus on spatial intelligence. His honors include a Royal Society Faraday Discovery Fellowship, the IEEE Thomas Huang Memorial Prize, the IEEE Mark Everingham Prize, two CVPR Best Paper Awards, and an ACM Test of Time Award. He is a Fellow of the Royal Academy of Engineering and of ELLIS, and has served as a chair of ECCV.

  • Rana Hanocka University of Chicago

    Rana Hanocka is an Assistant Professor of Computer Science at the University of Chicago and the Founder and CEO of Thrixel. She leads 3DL (threedle!), a group of researchers working on 3D, machine learning, and visual computing. She obtained her Ph.D. in 2021 from Tel Aviv University under the supervision of Daniel Cohen-Or and Raja Giryes. Her research builds artificial intelligence for 3D data, spanning computer graphics, machine learning, and computer vision, with the goal of bringing the success of deep learning on structured data to unstructured 3D geometry. Her group developed MeshCNN, a convolutional neural network designed specifically for meshes, and explored learning from the internal data of a single shape for surface reconstruction, geometric texture synthesis, and point cloud consolidation. Her current interests include intertwining human and machine creativity in 3D modeling and animation, learning with less supervision from large shape collections, and making 3D neural networks more interpretable.
